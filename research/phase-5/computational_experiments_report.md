# The Paradox of Absolute Luck
## Phase 5 — Computational Experiments, Identifiability, and Uncertainty

**Date:** 7 November 2026  
**Status:** **PASS — computational completion gate, 8/8**  
**Research format:** Gojo-style mathematical investigation: fictional motivation → coherent probabilistic constructions → adversarial tests → honest interpretations.  
**Scientific boundary:** All observations in this report are **synthetic Monte Carlo outcomes generated under stipulated mathematical distributions**. They are **not observations from the original WEBTOON**. No new probability theorem is claimed, and the comic-canon audit remains incomplete.

## Executive findings

1. **Collision (M1).** Two valid, globally coherent probability models make two players equally likely to defeat an ordinary player (99% each), but predict **50% versus 90%** for A's victory against B. Simulations reproduce both predictions. A stable weighted-choice mechanism is an additional structural assumption, not a consequence of high isolated win rates.
2. **Multiplication (M2).** Two exchangeable three-coin laws have the same single-coin success probability **70%** and the same two-coin joint success probability **50%**, yet differ in triple success (**30% versus 40%**). When observation is restricted to **only one or two coins per independent trial**, no amount of data can distinguish the two models; complete triple observations permit discrimination.
3. **200 coins (M2).** Three valid models with 99.5% reliability for each coin predict **0%, 36.6958%, or 49.8747%** all-coin success, depending on dependence and extendibility assumptions. Finite exchangeability must not be confused with infinite exchangeability.
4. **Allocation (M3).** Under independence, an equal boost maximizes all-three success against the concentrated allocation (**29.4080% versus 24.8750%**). With the **same fixed dependent joint mechanism used for both allocations**, the preference reverses (**33.0% versus 49.5%**). This strengthens Phase 4's original result, whose two extreme dependent constructions used different couplings for the two allocations.
5. **Uncertainty.** The 63 prespecified probability summaries passed the preregistered 4.5-SE-plus-small-floor accuracy diagnostics. A separate Wilson coverage calibration on five probability values yielded empirical coverage between **93.67% and 95.50%** across 600 synthetic datasets per value. Pointwise 95% coverage is not a simultaneous guarantee: **8 of 63** intervals did not contain their generating probability, without implying that the models failed.
6. **Reproducibility.** The complete Phase 2–4 regression chain passed in a temporary copy; the prior Phase 4 archive remained byte-identical; all six Phase 5 CSV/JSON result files and ten image outputs reproduced **byte for byte** within this software environment.

## 2. M1 — Two people who are both extraordinarily lucky

### Defining the two mechanisms

**Stable weighted choice (Bradley–Terry–Luce/Plackett–Luce).** With weights \(w_A=w_B=99\) and \(w_N=1\), tournament winners are generated by independent exponential clocks with rates proportional to weights. The probability of winning over any candidate set \(S\) is

\[
P(i\text{ wins over }S)=\frac{w_i}{\sum_{j\in S}w_j}.
\]

Consequently, A and B each beat ordinary N with probability 0.99, but A beats B with probability 0.50.

**Coherent latent-ranking countermodel.** Draw one full ranking from the following distribution:

| Ranking | Probability |
|---|---:|
| A > B > N | 0.89 |
| B > A > N | 0.10 |
| N > A > B | 0.01 |

The higher-ranked contestant wins any pairwise contest. This *single probability law* yields \(P(A>N)=P(B>N)=0.99\) but \(P(A>B)=0.90\). Its three-way winner is consistent with its same full ranking; it is not a patchwork of invented matchup-specific rates.

### Simulated results

| Model | Exact P(A beats B) | Monte Carlo estimate | 95% Wilson CI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Stable weights | 50.00% | 49.9875% | 49.7046–50.2704% |
| Latent ranking | 90.00% | 90.0167% | 89.8458–90.1850% |

See `figures/fig01_collision.svg` for the isolated and three-way contests.

**Interpretation:** Equal performance against a common baseline opponent does **not** identify the direct contest odds. “Both are 99% lucky” only becomes contradictory if both 99% claims apply to **the same mutually exclusive winning events in the same context**.

## 3. M2 — When knowing every pair cannot determine the triple

### Exact constructions

Let \(K=X_1+X_2+X_3\) be the number of successful coins. Conditional on \(K=k\), distribute the successes uniformly among the \({3\choose k}\) subsets. Both models are exchangeable:

| K successes | Model L | Model H |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0.10 | 0.00 |
| 1 | 0.00 | 0.30 |
| 2 | 0.60 | 0.30 |
| 3 | 0.30 | 0.40 |

For **both** models,

\[
P(X_i=1)=0.70,\qquad P(X_i=1,X_j=1)=0.50\quad(i\ne j).
\]

But the all-success rates are different:

\[
P_L(X_1=X_2=X_3=1)=0.30,\qquad P_H(X_1=X_2=X_3=1)=0.40.
\]

The complete two-coordinate law is also identical under both models, not just its first two moments: for any pair, \(P(11)=0.50\), \(P(10)=P(01)=0.20\), and \(P(00)=0.10\).

**Precise identifiability limit.** When every trial reveals **only one coin or one pair**, with fresh independent trials between measurements, the *entire distribution of observable records* is identical under L and H. No classifier can distinguish them better than chance on average under equal priors. This does **not** apply to coordinated pair records extracted from the same full triple: joint information across those records might reveal third-order dependence.

### Full-triple simulation

| Model | Exact all-three P | Simulated estimate | 95% Wilson CI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| L | 30.00% | 29.8600% | 29.6017–30.1196% |
| H | 40.00% | 39.9825% | 39.7057–40.2600% |

Even a single bit recording whether **all three** succeeded gives information unavailable under the one-/two-coordinate protocol.

### Discrimination with finite sample sizes

Predict H if the empirical rate of all-three success is at least **35%**; otherwise predict L. With \(n\) independent full-triple observations and threshold \(k=\lceil0.35n\rceil\), assuming equal model priors:

\[
\operatorname{Accuracy}(n)=1-\frac12\left[
P_{\mathrm{Bin}(n,0.30)}(K\ge k)
+P_{\mathrm{Bin}(n,0.40)}(K<k)\right].
\]

The exact binomial tails give:

| Trials per dataset | Exact expected accuracy | Replicated simulation accuracy |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 25 | 70.17% | 70.00% |
| 50 | 77.27% | 78.15% |
| 100 | 85.34% | 84.95% |
| 200 | 93.17% | 92.95% |
| 500 | 99.07% | 99.25% |
| 1,000 | 99.96% | 99.85% |

The classifier is not claimed to be Bayes optimal, and no learning/training optimization was performed. Each simulated accuracy uses 1,000 synthetic datasets from L and 1,000 from H. `figures/fig04_identifiability.svg` compares the exact accuracy against the repeated simulations.

## 4. M2 — The three versions of 200 almost-certain coins

Every model has the same success marginal for each coin, \(p=0.995\):

**Finite exactly-one-failure model.** Exactly one failure, with its index uniformly sampled among 200 positions. Thus \(P(\text{all succeed})=0\); the construction is finitely exchangeable but **not** extendible to a 201-variable exchangeable family preserving the original law.

**Independent model.** Each coin is a separate Bernoulli(0.995). Then

\[
P(\text{all succeed})=0.995^{200}\approx0.36695782.
\]

**Shared-latent model.** Draw \(\Theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(199,1)\); conditional on \(\Theta\), draw all 200 coin outcomes independently as Bernoulli(\(\Theta\)). Since \(E[\Theta]=199/200=0.995\), each marginal remains 99.5%, but

\[
P(\text{all succeed})=E[\Theta^{200}]=\frac{199}{399}\approx0.49874687.
\]

| Dependence structure | Exact P(all 200) | Simulation, 120,000 trials | 95% Wilson CI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Exactly one failure | 0.000% | 0.000% | 0–0.0032% |
| Fully independent | 36.696% | 36.568% | 36.296–36.841% |
| Shared Beta parameter | 49.875% | 49.753% | 49.470–50.035% |

A zero-event sample does not prove an unknown success probability must be zero. In this experiment, zero is known **by construction and proof**, not inferred merely from Monte Carlo nonoccurrence. Figure: `figures/fig02_joint_200_coins.svg`.

## 5. M3 — Same budget, different dependence, reversed optimum

All three coins have baseline success probability 0.5. Distribute a resource boost \(B=0.495\) so that \(p_i=0.5+b_i\), \(b_i\ge0\), \(\sum b_i=B\).

- **Equal:** \(p=(0.665,0.665,0.665)\).
- **Concentrated:** \(p=(0.995,0.500,0.500)\).

**Independent copula.** For independent coordinates, \(P(\text{all})=\prod p_i\). The equal allocation wins.

**Comonotonic copula.** With one shared uniform \(U\), all coordinates succeed when \(U\le p_i\); then \(P(\text{all})=\min_i p_i\). Equal still wins.

**Fixed mixed copula (counterexample under a common mechanism).** Use **one and the same dependence rule** for both allocations:

\[
X_1=\mathbf 1\{1-U\le p_1\},\quad
X_2=\mathbf 1\{U\le p_2\},\quad
X_3=\mathbf 1\{U\le p_3\},\qquad U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1).
\]

The all-success event is \(1-p_1\le U\le\min(p_2,p_3)\), so

\[
P(\text{all})=\max\{0,\min(p_2,p_3)-(1-p_1)\}.
\]

This gives 0.330 under equal allocation and 0.495 under concentrated allocation.

| Dependence mechanism | Equal: exact all-success | Concentrated: exact all-success | Preferred of these two |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Independent | 29.408% | 24.875% | Equal |
| Fully comonotonic | 66.500% | 50.000% | Equal |
| Fixed mixed copula | 33.000% | 49.500% | Concentrated |

The **fixed mixed copula** is a valid three-variable coupling, but an illustrative one; the original fictional ability does not establish that it operates under this rule. We do **not** claim that concentrated allocation is globally optimal among *all possible* allocations under that copula. We only show that a comparison ordering predicted by independence is not universal.

### Paired simulation of the fixed-mixed-copula reversal

Using **the same U in each trial** for both allocation strategies, the simulated difference, concentrated minus equal, was **+0.16485** (16.485 percentage points), compared with the exact **+0.16500**. The 95% paired large-sample interval was **[+0.16098,+0.16872]**, containing the theoretical value.

For comparison, using the same random inputs within each other mechanism produced paired theoretical differences of **−4.533** percentage points under independence and **−16.5** points under comonotonic dependence. The simulation matched both to prespecified accuracy tolerances.

**Important distinction from Phase 4:** Phase 4's largest 0% versus 50% contrast employed unrelated couplings in the two columns. That was sufficient to refute dependence-agnostic optimization, but it did not establish reversal when a coupling is held fixed. The new fixed mixed model directly addresses that concern.

Figure: `figures/fig03_allocation_dependence.svg`.

## 6. Monte Carlo uncertainty and coverage

### Wilson score interval

For \(k\) successes among \(n\) independent trials, set \(\hat p=k/n\), \(z=\Phi^{-1}(0.975)\), and define

\[
\mathrm{CI}_{\mathrm{Wilson}}=
\frac{\hat p+z^2/(2n)\ \pm\ z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n+z^2/(4n^2)}}{1+z^2/n}.
\]

This is a **pointwise approximate** 95% confidence interval and can under-cover for particular binomial probabilities. The independent audit checked every reported endpoint against SciPy's `binomtest(...).proportion_ci(method='wilson')`.

A coverage-calibration study used 600 independent binomial datasets of size 400 at each \(p\):

| True p | Observed Wilson coverage |
|---:|---:|
| 0.005 | 95.17% |
| 0.300 | 95.50% |
| 0.400 | 93.67% |
| 0.500 | 95.33% |
| 0.995 | 95.33% |

This is a finite simulated calibration, **not** a general proof that Wilson intervals always cover at least 95%.

Of the 63 event-rate intervals from the main runs, **8 did not include** the generating probability. The indicators are correlated within shared trials, and comparing 63 pointwise intervals is not an appropriate familywise 95% inference. All 63 rates nevertheless met the preregistered engineering accuracy tolerance. A reader should not interpret the audit's PASS as a claim that sampling noise vanished.

The convergence trace `figures/fig05_convergence.svg` shows raw absolute errors against the familiar \(O(n^{-1/2})\) reference scale. Individual error paths can fluctuate and are not guaranteed to decrease monotonically.

### Why our uncertainty assumptions are valid

Each **trial** is generated independently by the scenario-specific PCG64 stream. The 200 coins **within a trial** may exhibit arbitrary dependence or share a latent random variable. A statistic such as “all 200 succeeded in this trial” is still Bernoulli across independent trials with a fixed success probability, justifying binomial intervals for that indicator. Paired allocation contrasts keep within-trial dependence, estimate the paired difference, and use the trial-level sampling variance.

## 8. What these results do and do not establish

**Established under stipulated models:** exact probability constraints, mutually incompatible context-free contest guarantees, non-identifiability from one-/two-coin observations, different 200-coin collective predictions, and non-universality of equal allocation under dependence.

**Not established:** actual story activation probabilities, distribution of true comic Lucky Coin outcomes, empirical existence of the proposed shared-resource mechanism, a physically meaningful conserved “luck” quantity, or a newly discovered probability theorem.

**Interpretive insight for the Gojo-style essay:** a probability can appear almost absolute in isolation yet be powerless to predict a group outcome until the **event, sample space, observation protocol, and dependence structure** are specified. The mathematics does not fail; the informal idea of “absolute luck” lacks the information needed to determine a unique probability law.
